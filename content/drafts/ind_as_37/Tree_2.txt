Now, let's walk through how to determine the measurement of a provision. To start, we need to establish the "best estimate," which is essentially the amount the entity would rationally pay to settle the obligation or transfer it to another party as of the reporting date.

To find this amount, we first need to ask if we are dealing with a large population of items, such as product warranties, liability claims, or mass lawsuits. If the answer is yes, we use the Expected Value Method. This means we calculate the sum of the probability of each possible outcome multiplied by that outcome, ensuring we reflect the full range of possibilities. However, if we are dealing with a single obligation, we should instead focus on the most likely outcome, while still keeping other potential outcomes in mind.

Once we have our best estimate, we need to consider the time value of money. We ask ourselves if the effect of time is material, which is usually the case if the settlement is more than twelve months away. If it is material, we must discount the amount to its present value using a pre-tax risk-free rate that reflects the time value of money and the specific risks of that obligation. Just be careful here: do not use the entity's own credit risk in this calculation. As time passes, the "unwinding" of this discount is recorded as a finance cost in the profit and loss statement, separate from the original provision expense. If the time value of money isn't material, you can simply record the provision at the face value of your best estimate.

There are two important rules to remember during this process. First, if you expect any gains from disposing of an asset, do not use those gains to reduce the provision amount, even if they are related to the event. Second, you must review the provision at every balance sheet date. If the estimate changes, adjust it through the profit and loss statement. If the obligation is no longer probable, you should reverse the provision and record it as income.
